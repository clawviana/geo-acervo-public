My Silicon Valley Girl podcast has 1.56M subscribers on YouTube. I opened ChatGPT and asked which podcasts to follow about AI and startups. We weren't in the answer.

1.56M subscribers. None of them help when the question doesn't have my name in it.

Think about how that question used to work. We googled "best AI podcasts," got ten links, and clicked around. Now we ask ChatGPT instead, and it answers with a short list of names. No links. If your name isn't on it, you don't exist for that person.

This has a name: GEO, generative engine optimization. People keep treating it like SEO with a new label, and it isn't the same thing.

SEO has been around for decades and it's about ranking links in Google. GEO isn't three years old yet, the rules are still forming, and it decides whether AI says your name inside the answer at all.

So how does it choose? It names the shows, brands, and products that get written about most in other people's articles: the "15 best AI podcasts" lists, the directories, the Reddit threads, the write-ups in Forbes and Business Insider. It ranks purely on how often other people mention you. Quality barely enters into it.

ChatGPT answering "youtube business podcast." The list of sources gives an idea of where AI looks for answers.

The first thing I needed was to see where we actually stood, so I started with a tracker built for this: Peec.

The picture was worse than I expected. Across the 1,008 sources AI pulls from to answer that question for our category, we appeared in zero. Out of 49 category questions I tracked, we showed up in 5, every one tied to a specific guest's name. The same names won every time, none of them us.

The answers AI gave back on eight of those questions. The Position column is empty on every one, meaning our podcast didn't show up in any of them.

We set a plan to fix it. Results were not fast. Ten weeks in, the numbers finally started to move.

Visibility shows how often AI mentions us in its answers, and that went from 2.2% to 7.8%. But the number we were proud of was the other one, retrieved. It maps directly onto the work, because it only counts when a model opens our own site and uses what it finds. The site we built specifically for GEO had started doing its job, going from zero to 2.6%. How we built it is in week 2, below. These are still small numbers, but the direction is right.

The same stats, thirteen days apart. Visibility is how often AI names us at all. Retrieved is how often it opened our own site to write the answer.

Here's the plan, in the order that worked. Each week: what to do, why it matters, and how you'll know it worked. Anything technical sits in a PRO TIP marked for your developer. You'll see results without touching those.

 

Week 1 — Find out what AI says about you now

You can't fix what you haven't measured, and almost everyone measures the wrong thing.

Set up a tracker. I use Peec. A tool like this asks your questions to ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini and the rest every day, and records whether your name came up, how high, and how you were described. Add your name and any variations people use for you.

Then write 40 to 50 questions your ideal customer would actually type, and here's the part that matters: none of them can contain your name. "Best AI podcasts for founders," not "is Silicon Valley Girl good." A question with your name in it only confirms what someone already knew to look for. The broad question is the real test.

Part of the question set we track. These are the answers we're working to get into.

Run them and look. You'll probably find what I found: you appear when someone types your name, and vanish on every broad question where they don't.

Then look at which websites AI keeps quoting when it answers. Your tracker shows you this. For my category, 459 of the top 1,008 were "best of" list articles. That told me exactly where I needed to be.

By day 7 you have: a list of questions you want to win, and a list of websites you need to be on.

PRO TIP — fix your Wikidata
Wikidata is a free public database of plain facts ("Marina Mogilko, entrepreneur") that AI systems read to understand who you are, and anyone can edit it. Search for yourself. Ours existed but was labeled a YouTube channel, so AI thought we were a vlog and left us out of every podcast list. One wrong label there quietly spreads into how every model describes you.

 

Week 2 — Give AI something it can actually read

AI doesn't watch your videos and it doesn't listen to your audio. It reads text. The programs that do the reading are called crawlers: GPTBot for ChatGPT, PerplexityBot for Perplexity, ClaudeBot for Claude. They visit a page, read whatever text is sitting there, and move on. If your work only exists as a video, they find nothing to quote, and AI won't recommend what it can't quote.

Our podcast lives on YouTube. That's the main platform and it isn't changing. So we built a text layer on top of it: a page for every single episode, over at marinamogilko.co. Same content, in a form a crawler can read. Each page has the full transcript, a description that names the guest and their real title, the timestamps with labels, and a short bio.

Every episode gets a page here within hours of publishing. Almost nobody visits them. The crawlers do.

The transcript tab. This is what a crawler reads when it can't watch the video.

If you already have a website, your job this week is to make sure your best content is written out on it, not locked inside a video or a player. If you don't have one, this is the week you build something small. One page per episode, per product, per project. That's the whole requirement.

There's a quieter version of this that catches people who do have a website. If your words only appear after the page loads and runs its code, or they're tucked inside an embedded player, the crawler still arrives to a blank page and leaves. The text has to sit in the page itself. Getting our episode text to simply be there, in the page, did more for our numbers than any other single fix we made.

You can check your own in ten seconds. Right-click the page, choose "View page source," and search for a full sentence of your text. If it isn't in there, AI can't see it either.

View page source on one of our episode pages. The transcript is right there in the code, which is the only version a crawler ever gets.

You'll know it worked when: you can Google a full sentence from your transcript and your own page comes back. Your tracker will show it too, when "retrieved" starts to climb.

PRO TIP — keep it all on one web address
Look at where a word like "podcast" sits in the address. yoursite.com/podcast/ is a folder inside your site, and it inherits all the trust your site has built over the years. Choose this.
podcast.yoursite.com is treated as a brand-new separate site starting from zero, even though it looks almost the same. We had ours the second way and moved it to the first.

 

Week 3 — Match the words AI is already looking for

Here's something that isn't obvious. When you ask Perplexity "best AI podcasts," it doesn't search those three words. It quietly writes its own search queries first, things like "AI podcast CEO interviews 2026," and searches those instead. Those invented queries are called fanout, and your tracker shows you the exact ones the models generate.

So read them, then put those same words in your titles and descriptions.

We already had a clue about which words those would be. Remember the five questions we did win: every one of them had a guest's name in it. Nothing broad, only names. So we pushed names everywhere, and the job title with them: "LinkedIn CEO," "Head of Gemini." A clever, abstract title gives AI nothing to match against.

We try to lead every title with the company and the job, not just the guest's name. Those are the words the models are already searching for.

One warning that comes with automating this. Ours runs from Trint to Notion to Make.com to Claude and back, and it still gets names wrong. Guests, companies, product names. A misspelled name is a name AI can't match against, so someone reads every transcript before it goes live and fixes them. Skip that step and you quietly undo the work you just did on the titles.

Then publish more pages, and fix the ones you already have. Getting cited is partly a numbers game: the more pages you have using the right words, the more chances AI has to find you. Pick your best existing content and rewrite the titles and descriptions this week, a batch at a time, rather than waiting to do it perfectly.

You'll know it worked when: your new titles use the same words your tracker shows AI searching for.

PRO TIP — hand this to a developer
Add JSON-LD structured data to every content page, naming the people, dates, and roles inside it. It's an invisible block that tells a machine "this is a podcast episode, guest X, date Y" instead of making it guess.

 

Week 4 — Get onto the pages AI quotes

The fixes so far were one-time wins. This is the highest-return work you'll keep doing, and the one nobody wants to, because it isn't technical. It's asking.

Take that list of websites from week 1, the ones AI keeps quoting. For me those were podcast directories and "best of" roundups. One page alone got pulled into answers 70 times in a single analysis. Being on one of them beats anything you publish yourself, because AI trusts other people describing you more than you describing you.

So reach out, and reach out fast, within a day or two of publishing something, while it's still news. Lead with a fresh hook: one specific number or quote from your content they can't get anywhere else, and if a recognizable name is involved, put it first. "New interview with [well-known CEO], first data on X" opens doors that "please add my podcast" never will. Go for the outlets that get reprinted first, the big business ones, because one pickup there turns into ten.

Reddit, by hand. Find the threads AI quotes in your category, answer questions in them for real, get mentioned. A person writes every one of these. Automate it and you'll get the account banned.

Wikipedia, only if you qualify. Models trust it heavily, but you can't just write your own page. You need independent press already written about you, then you submit a draft through Articles for Creation and editors review it. Have the coverage? Do it. Don't? Skip it and come back later.

Then re-run your week 1 questions and compare. Report only the ones without your name in them. That's the honest number.

What you actually need

Four things: a tracker (Peec or similar), pages with your content written out as text, a Wikidata entry, and a way to publish regularly. My full stack is bigger. You don't need it.

Where this falls short

It's slow. You're waiting on other people's websites and on crawlers to come back around. Our numbers moved at ten weeks, not at day 30. The work fits in a month; the payoff doesn't.

Directories are easy to get into and barely move anything, because they list everyone. What moves the needle is a real article where a human chose to include you. Those are harder, and they're the point.
